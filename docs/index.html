Video Models Know Dynamics

An experiment log. Each experiment asks one question about whether video generation models carry an implicit model of physical dynamics, and shows the clips it produced so the claim can be checked rather than taken on trust.

The question behind the log#

A text-to-motion model learns from motion-capture sequences paired with captions. A video generation model learns from footage of the physical world. Both can be asked for "a person lifts a heavy box" — but only one of them has ever had to predict what light does to a body under load, how a torso braces, or how long a heavy thing takes to leave the ground.

The experiments here probe whether that difference in training signal shows up in the output: whether a video model's samples carry dynamics — weight, inertia, effort, contact — that a motion model's samples do not.

Experiments#

Exp 0
Generating motion with video models vs. a text-to-motion model
Given the same prompt, does a video model show the weight of an action where a text-to-motion model shows a generic version of it?
complete 2026-08-21 81 clips
Exp 2
Strong vs. loose control — how much motion does the video model add?
If we render a motion model's output as video and hand the video model only some of those frames, at what point does it stop copying and start inventing the motion itself?
complete 2026-08-23 132 clips
Exp 1
The round trip — does motion come back more natural than it went in?
Send a motion through the video model and pull it back out as motion. If the video model knows how bodies move, what comes back should look more natural than what went in — not identical to it.
complete 2026-08-23 144 clips
Exp 4
Photoreal output, animation as control
Earlier experiments made the video model hold an appearance it had never seen. If the animation is control only and the appearance comes from text, does the model spend its capacity on motion instead?
complete 2026-09-03 60 clips
Exp 6
Photoreal output, correct animation as control
Given a clean motion-capture clip as depth control and a sentence for appearance, how few conditioned frames still produce the right action, six seconds long, in a real place?
complete 2026-09-07 50 clips
Exp 7
Photoreal output, dense skeleton as control
Does a skeleton on every frame keep the pose while freeing the appearance — and does releasing the control early in denoising give the model more room?
complete 2026-09-11 90 clips
Exp 8
Photoreal output, partial skeleton as control
With the skeleton given on only K frames, where does the model stop copying the anchors, and where does the action stop being recognisable?
complete 2026-09-12 90 clips
Exp 9
Photoreal output, trajectory as control
If the model is told only where the body goes — a pelvis path, a few point tracks — and not how it moves, does the prompt and the video prior supply the action?
complete 2026-09-15 90 clips
Exp 10
Broken input, photoreal output, skeleton as control
Given a flawed text-to-motion clip as a skeleton on only K frames, does the video model redraw the bad frames in between into a better motion?
complete 2026-09-23 120 clips
Exp 11
Broken input, harder prompts, smarter anchors
On harder, object-heavy prompts where the text-to-motion input is clearly wrong, does the video model supply what is missing — and does picking the anchor frames smartly beat picking them evenly?
complete 2026-09-28 192 clips
Exp 12
Broken input — choosing keyframes by motion entropy
When does the choice of keyframes matter? Pick the frames where the body moves in the most directions at once, and see whether that recovers short key moments that evenly spaced frames miss.
complete 2026-09-28 360 clips
Exp 13
Broken input — changing how the control video is drawn
Exp 12's outputs followed their keyframes too loosely. Keep the keyframes fixed and change how the control video is built: does holding, chunking, a dense trunk or a full dense skeleton make the model follow — without just copying the flawed input?
complete 2026-10-01 528 clips
Exp 14
Broken input — trunk plus wrist and ankle dots
Exp 13's best control (trunk on every frame, full skeleton on keyframes) lost free-limb actions like kicks. Do wrist and ankle dots on every frame bring them back, and how many keyframes should the limbs get?
complete 2026-10-01 240 clips
Exp 15
Broken input — bad, uniform and hand-picked keyframes
Do keyframes picked by hand beat evenly spaced ones, are deliberately bad picks worse — and can a stage-by-stage prompt act as control on its own?
complete 2026-10-09 270 clips
Exp 16
Mocap input — bad, uniform and hand-picked keyframes
Repeat the keyframe ablation on real motion capture, where the true motion is known: does the choice of keyframes matter, and how much does a dense trunk signal add?
complete 2026-10-09 252 clips
Exp 17
Video model output as a condition for a motion model
Turn the pipeline around: take keyframes from a generated video and let a motion model (CondMDI) fill in between them. Is the result better 3D animation than the video's own pose lift?
complete 2026-10-09 120 motions
Why the numbering is out of order

They were numbered by when they were designed, not by when they run. Experiment 2 builds and validates the keyframe-conditioning machinery and chooses the two control settings; Experiment 1 then reuses that code, those settings and those files to close the loop. Reading 2 before 1 is much easier than the reverse.

Experiment 4 identified what was wrong with 1–3 — the blue character was doing two jobs at once — and fixed it. Experiment 6 then fixed the other problem those experiments shared: the control motion itself was often wrong, so it swaps MotionGPT3's output for clean motion capture. Experiments 3 and 5 are not written up here; 5 is the data-preparation step Experiment 6 consumes.

Experiments 7–17 are shorter write-ups. Each has an overview with the goal and the main findings, followed by pages of clips.

What the experiments add up to#

The state of the argument
  1. Keyframe conditioning works mechanically — pinned frames land where asked, full conditioning reproduces the input, and no morphing occurs. (Exp 2's checks)
  2. There is a real window where the video model re-times motion rather than copying it, and it exists for every prompt. (Exp 2's verdict)
  3. The round trip is essentially lossless, so motion added in the video survives back into 3D. (Exp 1's floor)
  4. The video prior extends; it does not correct. It can supply a missing part of an action — the extension of a punch, a box picked up and carried — and it cannot fix an action that is simply wrong for the prompt. (Exp 1's verdict)
  5. The control is a placement puzzle, not a dial. What the video model contributes is governed by where the free run sits, not by how many frames were withheld. (Exp 2)
  6. Separating "what to do" from "what to look like" removes the whole appearance confound. Feed the animation in as depth control and take appearance from text, and every output is a photoreal single person with the pose pinned to within about 80 mm wherever it is specified. (Exp 4)
  7. With a correct animation as control, two frames and a sentence produce the right action — six seconds of it, in a real place, for eight clips of ten. What sparsity costs is the particular performance, not the action. (Exp 6)
  8. And the model supplies physics the control does not have — a chair invented under a man who has to sit on something, squat repetitions replacing an implausible six-second hold, and foot contact cleaner than the motion capture's on every slow clip. (Exp 6's verdict)
What is still missing

Control. The model will give you the action; it will not give you your action. Every experiment so far has hit a ceiling that was a property of the control scheme rather than of the model — keyframe placement in Exp 2, blank-frame semantics in Exp 4, and in Exp 6 the fact that the frames carrying a specific performance are exactly the ones sparsity throws away.

Two concrete items are outstanding across the series: a control signal that is dense but weak rather than sparse and absolute, and a second camera — three experiments have now lost a clip to a single fixed viewpoint on a floor-level pose.

How to read these pages#

Conventions used throughout

Every experiment is split into short pages: what the question was, how it was set up, what to watch for, then one page per prompt carrying nothing but that prompt's clips, then an Observations page holding the discussion and the numbers, then a verdict and provenance.

The split is deliberate. A result page is meant to go on a projector — headings that name the conditioning set and the frame indices, and the clips underneath. Everything that needs reading rather than watching lives on the Observations page.

Picking a page hides the sidebar, so the clips get the whole window. The button at the top left, or n, brings it back.

Clips autoplay muted. They loop in lockstep rather than independently — one timer restarts every visible clip together, so a comparison stays frame-aligned instead of drifting apart after a few seconds. Where the arms differ in length, the shorter one holds on its last frame until the group comes round again.

The toolbar at the top of a results page switches seed, runs the whole page at ¼×, ½×, 1× or 2×, restarts everything from t = 0, and turns the lockstep off if you want it. Click any clip to open it full-size and step through it a frame at a time. Click a timing strip to open it full-size and scroll it.

Comparisons are always laid out so that the thing being compared sits inside one block. Where a prompt has a matched heavy/light counterpart, the two are adjacent rows of the same grid; where a returned motion has a source video, they are adjacent cells. Comparing across blocks is not meaningful: the arms differ in resolution, frame rate, clip length and visual style by construction, and those differences are documented per experiment rather than normalised away.

Keyboard shortcuts

n show or hide the sidebar · / focus the sidebar filter · r replay all visible clips in sync · p pause or play all · click a clip to open it · ← → step one frame · space play/pause · Esc close.
