Photoreal output, correct animation as control

Experiment 6 · how little pose is enough?

Given a clean motion-capture clip as depth control and a sentence for appearance, how few conditioned frames still produce the right action?

  • Earlier experiments used a motion model's output as the control. It often did not perform the action in the prompt at all, so we could never separate did the video model improve the motion from can it rescue a motion that was never right.
  • Here the control is clean motion capture — ten AMASS/BABEL clips that genuinely perform the action they are labelled with.
  • The animation is control only. We render it as a depth video and feed that in. Appearance comes from the text prompt, so the output is a photorealistic person in a real place.
  • Then we pull the motion back out with pose recovery and re-render it on the same neutral body, so the motion that came back can be compared with the motion that went in.
  • The one variable is how many frames carry a pose — 22 of them down to 2. Everything else is held fixed.

This is the "a video model behaves like a renderer when you specify every frame" point, showing up in appearance rather than in motion.

What the sweep shows

For eight of the ten clips the action survives all the way down to two conditioned frames. What sparsity costs is not what the person does but when and where — the input's trajectory, its timing, its specific stride.

Where copying breaks
  • K = 8 — the fast, high-frequency clips: jump, kick, throw
  • K = 5 — the locomotion clips: walk, run, stand up
  • never sharply — the slow or near-static ones: sit down, turn, squat, wave. They just drift.
The model supplies physics the control lacks
  • sit down — no chair in the control, no chair in the prompt. It puts a stool under him at every density and keeps him on it.
  • squat at K = 2 — one six-second hold becomes repetitions, which is what a person in a gym actually does.
  • Foot contact beats the motion capture on every slow clip: wave 22.6 → 1.3 mm/s, squat 39.8 → 10.4, sit down 40.8 → 13.7.
Where it goes wrong
  • run — 5–10× the input's foot skate at every sparse density. It knows what running looks like, not where this runner's feet were.
  • throw — nine times the input's jerk at K = 3, and a tennis racket at every density because the prompt named no ball.
  • stand up — pose recovery struggles with a body prone on the floor. Third experiment to hit this; the fix is a second camera.

Two frames plus a sentence is enough to produce the right action, six seconds long, in a real place. It is not enough to reproduce a particular performance.
